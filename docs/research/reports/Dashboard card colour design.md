# One formula, not five hues, signals premium

The colour choice that separates a "classic/premium" admin dashboard from a "cheap" one is not which five hues you pick — it is whether every hue is built and deployed through **one shared, restrained formula**: a light, desaturated tint for the fill, a markedly darker and more saturated shade of the *same* hue reserved for the icon/text/accent, and that exact hue reused identically everywhere it appears for one domain, never drifting and never doubling as a status colour. This formula is independently reinvented by GitHub's Primer, Radix Colors, and Tailwind-ecosystem libraries like Tremor — three unrelated, real, shipped design systems that converge on the identical shape (light bg + dark same-hue fg) despite using different colour spaces and different numbering schemes ([Primer primitives](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/primer/primitives/main/src/tokens/component/label.json5), [Radix Colors](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors/docs/palette-composition/understanding-the-scale), [Tremor Badge](https://tremor.so/docs/ui/badge)). Named critics of bad dashboard colour — most authoritatively Stephen Few — converge on the opposite diagnosis of what goes wrong: not "too much colour" per se, but colour whose meaning doesn't stay fixed across the screen, or that's applied decoratively with no informational payoff ([Perceptual Edge](https://www.perceptualedge.com/blog/?p=154)). Five business-domain colours sits comfortably inside every numeric ceiling researchers have proposed for quick, confident recognition — roughly 5 to 7 — so a five-card dashboard is well within budget, provided red and green are left alone, since every serious system in this research reserves them for status (error/success), and a sixth "mixed/no domain" bucket should fall back to neutral grey rather than inventing a sixth hue. For light, dark, and a third "slate" theme, the correct method is not picking three independent sets of five hex values, but anchoring each domain hue to a fixed step on one perceptually-structured 10–12-step ramp per hue, and having each theme simply read a different step for the same role — exactly how Radix, Material Design 3, and Carbon all handle dark mode today.

## Restraint applied consistently is the actual premium signal, not any one hue

The single most repeated, most concrete claim across every tier of source is that premium products **tint their neutrals with a trace of one hue rather than using pure gray/white/black**, and that this — not the brand colour itself — is "the one color decision" argued to separate a UI that "feels designed" from one that "feels assembled," with Linear, Stripe, Vercel, and Raycast named as examples ([UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/the-one-color-decision-that-makes-a-ui-look-expensive-d6890efe11ba)). Refactoring UI's widely-paraphrased guidance gives the mechanical version of the same idea: build each colour as a 5–9-step tonal scale rather than a flat swatch, never drop to literal `#000000`/`#FFFFFF`, and tint grays with a consistent undertone — blue for a cool UI, yellow/brown for a warm one ([Refactoring UI summary](https://smithery.ai/skills/wondelai/refactoring-ui)). This generalizes directly to a multi-hue categorical system: the risk is not that five domain colours exist, it's that if four are muted and restrained while a fifth is left saturated, the "deliberate restraint" signal breaks — and *that specific inconsistency*, not the presence of colour, is what reads as cheap. Evil Martians' Harmonizer tool makes the perceptual-uniformity version of this explicit, building palettes in OKLCH specifically so that "same-level" colours share lightness and contrast across every hue, rather than the hue-dependent brightness mismatches that plain HSL produces — yellow and blue at an identical HSL lightness value look wildly different in actual brightness, while OKLCH is designed so a fixed lightness step looks the same regardless of hue ([Evil Martians Harmonizer](https://evilmartians.com/opensource/harmonizer); [UX Design, OKLCH explained](https://uxdesign.cc/oklch-explained-for-designers-dc6af4433611)). The directly buildable rule this yields: constrain every one of your five domain hues to the *same* OKLCH lightness value (or a tight, deliberately-reused 2–3-step band per UI role — one lightness for "icon tint," a different one for "accent border") and the same chroma band — e.g. all five sitting in a muted-but-identifiable 0.08–0.14 chroma range rather than one colour at 0.20 and another at 0.03 ([Preline palette guidance](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/preline@4.2.0/skills/theme-generator/docs/palette-guidance.md)). Separately, a real consumer-research study cited by name — not just a design blogger's opinion — found people paid more for an identical product in a highly saturated colour than a muted one, which is the one place in this research where "saturation reads as more expensive" has an actual citation behind it, though the study is about a single product's colour, not a multi-hue categorical system, and the direction of the effect by category/context is not pinned down ([Oprah.com, citing *Journal of Consumer Research*](https://www.oprah.com/home/how-to-stop-overspending)).

## The industry's real formula: a light tint fill, a saturated same-hue accent, never the reverse

Strip away the marketing language and every credible badge/tag/card source in this research describes the identical two-part construction, independently, in three real production systems. GitHub's Primer defines 19 named hues for its Label component, each with a background drawn from the lightest step of that hue's scale and a foreground text colour from a notably darker, more saturated step six positions up — with **no border at all**, contrast carried purely by the bg/fg pair — and every value is separately resolved for light and dark mode from the same named scale ([Primer primitives — component tokens](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/primer/primitives/main/src/tokens/component/label.json5)). Radix Colors documents the identical shape with numbered, role-assigned steps: step 3 for a coloured UI element's resting background, step 6 or 7 for its border, and step 11 (low-contrast) or step 12 (high-contrast) for its text — each with an explicit APCA contrast guarantee against the matching step-2 background, in both themes ([Radix "Understanding the scale"](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors/docs/palette-composition/understanding-the-scale)). Tremor's open-source Tailwind Badge component applies the same logic in Tailwind's own vocabulary: a `-50` background paired with `-900` text in light mode, flipping in dark mode to a low-opacity `-400/10` background with mid-brightness `-400` text ([Tremor Badge docs](https://tremor.so/docs/ui/badge)). Independent accessibility guidance explains *why* this direction and not the reverse: a badge/pill convention of light tint background plus dark, saturated text is explicitly preferred because a saturated *background* with light text becomes "extremely hard to read" at small sizes, and a GitHub Shields accessibility issue documents this as a named, stated reason rather than an aesthetic preference ([MiHCM badge accessibility](https://designsystem.mihcm.com/components/badge/accessibility); [GitHub Shields issue #4661](https://github.com/badges/shields/issues/4661)). This same tint-for-fill/saturation-for-accent split scales up cleanly from a text badge to a full card: a soft-tinted icon chip (a light background at roughly 5–10% opacity of the brand hue) holding a fully-saturated solid icon glyph reproduces the exact same pattern at card-header scale, concentrating the eye's attention on the one small saturated element rather than spreading saturation across the whole card surface — precisely the technique IBM Carbon's own data-viz docs describe as "deliberate hits of color... combined with rich neutrals for a balanced, cohesive experience" ([Carbon data-viz colour palettes](https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/)). Full-saturation solid fills, across every source checked, are reserved for things that are rare on screen and demand action — primary buttons, focus rings, a single brand accent — never for a category marker repeated across five cards and every row of a merged table, which is exactly the opposite of how a saturated fill should be spent ([Shopify Polaris visual design](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/design/visual-design)).

## Five domains is comfortably inside every ceiling — but red and green are already taken

Every numeric source on how many simultaneous categorical colours a reader can hold converges on a band of roughly **3 to 8**, trending toward the low end for anything meant to be scanned quickly rather than analyzed. Named colour researcher Maureen Stone proposed 5–7 distinct, memorable colours as the practical range; Miller's classic "magic number 7" for working-memory capacity is the most commonly cited explanation for why that band exists at all; Tamara Munzner's rule of thumb runs 6–12, with the caveat that text, gridlines, and background colours already eat into that budget ([via Jon Schwabish's analysis](https://jschwabish.substack.com/p/why-are-six-colors-common-in-color)). Adobe Spectrum's own shipped categorical data-viz palette caps at 6, with a stated claim — though uncited by Adobe itself — that comprehension becomes "more difficult starting at 6... extremely difficult... at 12" ([via Schwabish, citing Spectrum](https://jschwabish.substack.com/p/why-are-six-colors-common-in-color)). On the stricter end, performance-dashboard consultant Stacey Barr recommends 3–5 colours total *including* black and grey ([Stacey Barr](https://www.staceybarr.com/?p=5010)), and a FusionCharts guide puts the "looks lit like the Christmas tree" threshold at just 3 ([FusionCharts, via summary](https://www.fusioncharts.com/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/16628)). Five domain colours — Payments, Meters, Installations, Complaints, Demos, to use this project's own founders'-dashboard shape — sits inside every one of these ranges without strain, and only risks trouble if a sixth or seventh domain is bolted on later without revisiting the scheme.

The sharper, more actionable constraint is which two hues to leave alone. Across every semantic-colour source found — a community design-systems reference, Atlassian's token architecture, Carbon's dedicated Alert palette, and Citi's Salt design system's own status tokens — red and green (and, to a lesser extent, orange/amber) are universally reserved for error/success/warning meaning, and reusing them for an unrelated category creates exactly the failure mode Stephen Few names explicitly in his review of a real enterprise dashboard: the same colour meaning different things in different places on one screen, training the viewer to infer a relationship that doesn't exist ([Perceptual Edge, Stephen Few](https://www.perceptualedge.com/blog/?p=154)). Atlassian's own design-token documentation states the rule directly — "don't use an accent when the color already has semantic meaning" — and structures its system so a raw hue primitive is only ever aliased to one fixed semantic token ([Atlassian tokens docs](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@atlaskit/tokens@13.0.4/tokens.docs.tsx)). Tellingly, even Carbon's own 14-colour categorical sequence includes a red and a green among its "neutral-meaning" categories with no documented defense of the overlap, and Citi's Salt system lets its sentiment palette (Negative/Positive/Caution) literally share hex values with its status palette (Error/Success/Warning) without ever flagging or arguing for the coincidence ([Carbon categorical palette](https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/); [Salt Design System](https://www.saltdesignsystem.com/salt/foundations/color/semantic-use-of-color)) — the clear, convergent implication across every source, even where it's not stated outright, is that a purely arbitrary domain-label system (no card here is inherently "good" or "bad") has no such "close enough in meaning" excuse and should avoid red/green cleanly, routing any genuine good/bad/urgent signal *within* a card through those two reserved hues instead, exactly as this project's existing `StatusChip` tones already do. Where a sixth, miscellaneous, or "spans every domain" row needs a treatment, the right answer per this research is a neutral grey chip, not a sixth invented hue — a community design-system reference explicitly recommends grey as the fallback "when unsure, since grey conveys status without carrying semantic weight" ([via community skill reference](https://uxplanet.org/the-one-color-decision-that-makes-a-ui-look-expensive-d6890efe11ba)).

## Dark and slate themes reuse one ramp; they don't invent a second palette

Of the systems that document a dark-mode derivation method at all, none of them hand-pick dark-mode hex values hue by hue — every one **anchors each hue to a fixed position in an underlying, perceptually-structured scale and lets each theme select a different step number for the same role**. Material Design 3 generates its tonal palettes from a single seed colour in HCT (Hue, Chroma, Tone) space and handles dark mode by *inverting which tone number serves which role* — a light theme uses a high-tone background with low-tone content, a dark theme flips that assignment (e.g. tone-80 content on a tone-10/20 background), while the underlying hue and chroma stay anchored to the exact same seed-derived palette ([corroborated by a Cordova dynamic-colour plugin's API docs](https://www.mintlify.com/ollm/cordova-plugin-dynamic-color/api/palette)). Carbon's categorical palette does the mechanically identical thing on its own 10/100-step colour ramp: "Purple" in dark mode isn't a new colour, it's the same hue family's step 60 instead of light mode's step 70 ([via a third-party Carbon-based package](https://socket.dev/npm/package/@polyxd/ds-carbon)); for sequential (not categorical) scales Carbon states the rule even more plainly — "in dark themes, the lightest color denotes the largest values," the literal inverse of the light-mode convention ([Carbon data-viz colour palettes](https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/)). Radix Colors ships this as a pre-built pair of scales per hue — a light scale and a separately-authored, identically-named dark scale, switched with a single class on a container — with the one documented hard constraint that every step-11/12 text colour passes an APCA contrast guarantee against its own theme's step-2 background ([Radix Colors](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors)). No source in this research documents a method specific to a *third*, "slate" theme distinct from the ordinary light/dark pair — this is a genuine gap in the published literature, not a solved problem borrowed from elsewhere — but the generalizable method clearly extends: pick the OKLCH (or HCT) step/tone that gives each of the five domain hues the correct apparent weight against slate's specific navy-chrome ground, using the identical "fixed role, different step per theme" logic rather than hand-picking a third independent set of five hex values.

## A concrete, borrow-ready five-domain palette, assembled from systems that already shipped one

This is also the moment to apply Carbon's "check the *order* for adjacent-category confusability" lesson: Carbon's own 14-colour sequence is deliberately ordered Purple→Cyan→Teal→Magenta→Red specifically so neighbouring categories never read as the same hue family at a glance, which is directly checkable for any five-card row layout before shipping it ([Carbon data-viz colour palettes](https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/)).

## Conclusion

The research converges on a genuinely usable, non-obvious answer: the "classic/premium vs. cheap/garish" axis in a categorical colour system is not primarily a *hue-selection* problem, it's a *formula-discipline* problem. A five-hue system built with light-tint-fill-plus-saturated-same-hue-accent, held to the same lightness and chroma band across every hue, kept off red/green, and derived for dark/slate by moving to a different step on the same ramp rather than inventing new values, will read as deliberate and restrained regardless of which five specific hues are chosen — while even a tasteful hue selection will read as assembled the moment one domain breaks the shared formula (a bolder fill, a mismatched chroma, a borrowed status colour). The corollary worth carrying forward is Stephen Few's: the actual enemy of a "premium" dashboard isn't colour volume at all, it's colour whose meaning silently drifts across the screen — so the single highest-leverage check before shipping a five-card, colour-coded dashboard with a merged table underneath it is not "do these five colours look nice together," but "does this exact hue mean this exact domain everywhere it appears, and nowhere else."
