# Celebrate stars publicly, warn defaulters privately

**Build the "star societies" half and do not build the public "defaulter societies" half.** The evidence on public naming points one way: most of the change in payment comes from the *threat* of being named, which can be delivered privately. Publishing the list adds little, and the gain fades quickly. Recognition did about twice as much as disclosure in the best natural experiment found. Rankings that show a visible bottom made low performers withdraw rather than improve.

No mainstream platform publishes a "worst customers" list. Uber, Airbnb, Upwork and the Indian housing-society apps keep negative customer signals private, or limit them to the people about to deal with that customer. Under Indian law, a "defaulter" label is defensible only when the debt is true, undisputed and current, and published for a real public good. Showing one society's debt to other societies is weak on public good. A "how staff treat our field executives" rating is the hardest part of the idea to defend, and the Kerala High Court has already ordered a public defaulter board taken down as coercive. The defaulter list in the portal's shared rotating section is the part most likely to cause trouble.

The closest safe version of your goal has three parts:
- a positive, opt-in star showcase scored against a published standard, not a rank;
- a stats strip of aggregate figures ("most societies pay on time");
- each late society's own status and the published listing rule shown privately to its committee, with consequences that are real but enforced through the contract and the law, not through peers.

All of this builds on the defaulter flag, arrears clock and maker-checker approval you already have.

## Most of the effect comes from the warning, not the public list

The strongest study separates the two effects. Slovenia announced a tax-debtor list four months before publishing it. During those four months, **corporations cut their tax debt by about 8.5% and individuals by about 5%**, with the largest effect in reputation-sensitive industries. When the list was actually published, it added a further effect that was "marginal in terms of revenue and tapers off quickly" ([CEPR DP13194](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP13194.pdf); [VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/shaming-and-tax-compliance)).

A US field experiment found the same pattern. About 34,000 tax delinquents were sent letters warning them they could be listed. Making the list more visible moved small debtors (under about $2,500) and did nothing measurable for larger ones. Telling debtors how their neighbours were doing had no effect at all ([NBER w21264](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21264); [MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2015/04/15/should-we-publicly-shame-tax-delinquents)). The exact effect sizes differ between versions of that paper, so treat the 20% figure quoted in the press as indicative.

The UK tax authority publishes deliberate defaulters owing £25,000 or more. Its own evaluation found weak deterrence, because **only 28% of people knew the scheme existed, against 70% who knew about fines**. Some of those named never noticed they had been named ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/evaluating-the-effectiveness-of-publishing-details-of-deliberate-defaulters); [Tax Journal](https://taxjournal.com/articles/publishing-deliberate-defaulters-not-effective-deterrent-25072018)).

A meta-analysis of 45 randomised trials found that a plain reminder lifts payment by about 2.7 percentage points. A message stating the consequences adds about 3.2 points, more than a social-norm message adds ([ZEW](https://www.zew.de/PU86278)).

For FirsThing, the people likely to respond are societies that are late through drift: a missed approval, or a committee handover. A society in a real dispute will not move. A public list therefore gets you little that a firm, well-timed private warning would not, and it carries all the downside.

Recognition does better than shaming. Pakistan published every taxpayer's tax paid, and at the same time honoured the top 100 payers in each category. **Disclosure raised taxes paid by about 9 log points; recognition by about 17** ([NBER w25623](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25623); [CEPR DP14463](https://cepr.org/publications/dp14463)).

How the recognition is given matters, in three ways:
- **A rank backfires.** In a Zambian health-worker trial, public appreciation raised performance, but adding a rank against peers *lowered* it, mostly among weaker performers, who held back effort rather than learn where they stood ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/publications/Awards-unbundle_Science_January2014.pdf)).
- **Surprise awards backfire.** In a trial with 15,329 California students, surprise attendance awards *reduced* later attendance. The award signalled that turning up was unusual rather than expected ([HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/demotivating-effect-and-unintended-message-awards-0)).
- **Comparison alone can pull good performers down.** Telling households how they compared with neighbours made below-average energy users *increase* their usage. Adding a simple sign of approval (a smiley) stopped this "boomerang" ([Schultz et al. reprise](https://www.scranton.edu/faculty/nolan/pdfs/social-norms-reprise_perspectives_schultz-et-al2018.pdf)).

A UK trial with more than 200,000 taxpayers shows what does work cheaply: telling late payers that "nine out of ten people pay on time" raised payment at no cost ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/aug14/w20007.html)).

So the star section works best as a statement that **paying on time is normal and appreciated**, judged against a fixed standard. A top-five list implies that stars are rare, and it creates a bottom that someone must occupy.

Your audience adds a further problem. India's Green Rating Project found that rated mills cut pollution where their own communities could put pressure on them ([RFF](https://www.rff.org/publications/working-papers/does-disclosure-reduce-pollution-evidence-from-indias-greenrating-project/)). For a housing society, the people who can pressure a slow committee are its own residents. A portal home page shown to *other* societies' committees reaches the wrong audience. A committee that changes every year may also be named for its predecessor's lapse. No study was found on naming volunteer-run housing bodies, so this point rests on inference.

## Nobody comparable publishes a "worst customers" wall

Large two-sided platforms do rate their customers, but they keep the rating private:
- **Uber** shows riders their own score. Since 2019 it removes riders who fall well below their city's average, but only after "several notifications and opportunities to improve", and it never published the threshold ([WEF](https://weforum.org/agenda/2019/05/uber-will-start-deactivating-riders-with-low-ratings)).
- **Upwork** shows freelancers a client's verified payment method and past feedback before they apply. That information reaches only the counterparty about to deal with the client ([Upwork](https://www.upwork.com/resources/upwork-client-profiles-what-you-need-to-know)).
- **Airbnb** comes closest to public customer reviews. Each review must be tied to a real stay, both sides' reviews are published together so neither can retaliate, and the person reviewed can post a public reply ([Airbnb Help](https://www.airbnb.co.in/help/article/13/how-do-reviews-work)).

None of these platforms publishes a list of its worst customers.

India's own debt mechanisms follow the same pattern:
- **CIBIL MSME Rank** is shown to lenders, not the public ([Muthoot Finance](https://www.muthootfinance.com/blog/what-is-cmr-understanding-cibil-msme-rank-and-its-importance)).
- **MSME Samadhaan** did not publicly name defaulting buyers ([ClearTax](https://news.cleartax.in/cimsme-launches-portal-for-reporting-payment-defaults-to-msmes/7536/amp)).
- **NoDefaulters.com** lists a buyer only after contacting it and giving it an agreed period to pay. The listing is shown only to registered users, and the buyer can respond ([ClearTax](https://news.cleartax.in/cimsme-launches-portal-for-reporting-payment-defaults-to-msmes/7536/amp)).
- **Surat's textile federation (FOSTTA)** verifies a complaint before it lists a distributor ([Textile Excellence](https://textileexcellence.com/single-news/6697/payment-crisis-surat-based-fostta-to-list-defaulters-publicly)).
- **The RBI's wilful-defaulter process** is the most formal. It applies only to dues of ₹25 lakh or more. An identification committee decides first, a separate review committee then hears the borrower's written reply, and a photograph can be published only under a board-approved policy ([Mondaq](https://mondaq.com/india/financial-services/1513042/directions-for-treatment-of-wilful-and-large-defaulters)).

Housing-society apps such as MyGate and ADDA show a committee which of *its own residents* owe it money. No app or vendor was found that ranks societies against each other ([MyGate](https://mygate.com/blog/innovation/penalise-late-payments-incentivize-payers); [ADDA support](https://support.adda.io/portal/en/kb/articles/how-do-i-put-up-the-list-of-maintenance-fee-defaulters-on-the-notice-board)).

Positive B2B recognition, by contrast, is common and safe because the customer opts in, as with case studies. Gartner's "Customers' Choice" adds a minimum-evidence rule: at least 50 reviews and an average of at least 4.2 before anyone is recognised ([Solutions Review](https://solutionsreview.com/marketing-automation/gartner-names-december-peer-insight-customer-choice-crm-lead-management)).

The finding that matters most is that **no case was found of an Indian B2B service company publicly shaming its own customers**. A cross-society defaulter wall would go further than anything comparable. Nobody has tested how that plays out, and no case shows that it is lawful.

## Indian law tolerates a true debt statement, not a peer shaming board

*This section is research, not legal advice. Several primary texts could not be retrieved; get a lawyer to confirm before launch.*

**Defamation.** Defamation is now section 356 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, which replaced IPC 499/500 in July 2024. Its Explanation 4 says that an imputation lowering a person's *credit* harms their reputation, and "defaulter" goes straight to credit ([devgan.in](https://devgan.in/bns/section/356/)). A truth defence needs two things: the statement must be true, and publishing it must be for the public good ([legalserviceindia](https://www.legalserviceindia.com/Legal-Articles/defamation-under-bhartiya-nyaya-sanhita/)).

**Who can sue.** The law extends defamation to "an association or collection of persons", provided it is an identifiable body ([Indian Kanoon](https://future.indiankanoon.org/doc/10089)). A registered society with a name and address clearly is one. Any committee member named or implicated could also sue in their own right.

**How the cases come out.** The cases that went the creditor's way all rest on proven dues:
- In 2026 the Bombay High Court refused to restrain HDFC Bank from calling a trust a wilful defaulter, because the material on record showed the sums were due. It awarded ₹5 lakh in costs against the trust ([Verdictum](https://www.verdictum.in/bombay-high-court/lilavati-kirtilal-mehta-trust-v-hdfc-bank-limited-interim-application-no3095-of-2025-media-campaign-not-retaliatory-defamation-1615735)).
- The Allahabad High Court took a hard view of bank officials who kept showing a borrower as a defaulter after his loan was closed ([Moneylife](https://moneylife.in/article/allahabad-high-court-asks-icici-bank-chairman-to-explain-use-of-recovery-agents-despite-supreme-court-bar/74247.html)).
- In December 2024 the Kerala High Court upheld removal of a co-operative's flex board listing about 1,750 loan defaulters with their photographs. It held that display is not a lawful way to recover money, and that coercing payment through threats to reputation offends privacy and dignity ([SCC Online](https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2024/12/26/borrowers-coerced-repayment-threats-reputational-damage-privacy-invasion-kerala-hc/); [IndiaLaw](https://indialaw.in/blog/civil/public-shaming-loan-defaults-kerala-hc-no)).

Your stated goal, to make societies aware that misbehaving gets them highlighted, is exactly the "pressure through reputation" purpose that the Kerala court rejected. If a court reads a listing as a collection lever, the public-good and good-faith defences weaken.

**Disputed and partly paid invoices.** Your own billing has many of these: light-count disagreements, the basis of the savings figure, payments made net of TDS, and invoices paid before the system existed. Any of them can turn a true "defaulter" claim into a false one.

**Staff-behaviour ratings.** A rating built on your own executives' impressions mixes provable fact ("refused our technician entry on 12-10") with opinion. Indian courts protect opinion only when it rests on true facts and carries no malice ([LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/amp/news-updates/a-statement-is-not-true-only-because-online-bombay-hc-144748)). Free text typed by a back-office user is where unprovable claims creep in.

**Data protection.** A society is not a person, so naming only the society sits mostly outside the DPDP Act. Committee members' names, photos, or comments attributed to identifiable staff are personal data. They were collected for billing, and using them for a rating needs fresh, specific consent, which a "defaulter" society's members will not give. The DPDP Rules were notified on 13 November 2025, and their notice and consent duties apply from about May 2027 ([AZB & Partners](https://www.azbpartners.com/bank/indias-digital-personal-data-protection-act-phased-rollout-and-key-compliance-milestones/)). The Article 21 privacy argument from the Kerala case applies today.

**No platform protection.** Because your staff approve and write each listing, FirsThing is the publisher, not a platform hosting other people's content. It should not expect the IT Act's intermediary safe harbour (lawyer to confirm).

**Due process.** For regulated lenders, the Supreme Court in *SBI v. Jah Developers* held that a defaulter label hits the right to carry on business. It requires a reasoned draft order, a 15-day window to respond, and a reasoned decision by a review committee ([Casemine](https://www.casemine.com/in/column/the-legal-framework-of-wilful-default-in-india/view)). That rule binds banks, not FirsThing, but it is the yardstick a court will reach for when it asks whether a vendor acted in good faith.

**The lawful levers you have instead.** If FirsThing is registered as a micro or small enterprise, the MSMED Act gives it more than any list would:
- buyers must pay within at most 45 days;
- late payment carries compound interest at three times the RBI bank rate, which cannot be contracted out of;
- disputes go to the MSE Facilitation Council for conciliation, then arbitration decided within 90 days ([LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/amp/law-firms/law-firm-articles-/the-stringency-of-interest-provisions-under-msmed-act-implementation-and-judicial-interpretation-310929); [iPleaders](https://blog.ipleaders.in/?p=130194)).

Whether a housing society counts as a "buyer" under that Act, and whether FirsThing holds an Udyam registration, both need checking. Your contract's suspension clause, which the arrears clock already drives, is a second lawful lever.

## What to build, part by part

The verdicts below keep everything in your idea that the evidence supports and replace the rest with the closest safer version.

| Part of the idea | Verdict | Closest safe version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Star societies shown to all societies | Keep, opt-in | Show a society only after its committee agrees in writing. Recognise it against a stated standard, not by rank. |
| Defaulter societies shown to all societies | Drop | Show each society only its own status, plus an anonymous aggregate ("17 of 19 societies are fully paid"). Keep the named defaulter list in the back office, where it is today. |
| Score includes "is a defaulter" | Change | Make the defaulter status a factual rule, never a low composite score. |
| Score includes staff treatment of field executives | Drop from any published view | Keep it as an internal incident log handled privately with the committee. |
| More parameters later | Keep, carefully | Add only measurable parameters, each needing a minimum amount of data. |
| System flags, back office approves each | Keep and strengthen | Use your existing maker-checker flow, with a notice and reply step for anything negative. |
| Manual addition by back office | Keep for stars only | Put it through the same approval queue, with "manual" as the reason. |
| Toggle descriptive text | Keep, templated | Generate the text from data. Allow no free typing on a published card. |
| Auto-scrolling carousel | Change | Use a static grid on desktop and a swipe row on phones. If it must rotate, give it a Pause control and stop it for reduced-motion users. |
| Stats strip with links to full lists | Keep | Show aggregate figures, each with an as-of date. Link to the full star list only. |

### Scoring that survives a challenge

Copy the shape of commercial credit scores without copying their secret weights:
- **Per invoice.** Measure days late on the day the last rupee arrived, and count TDS as paid when it is recorded.
- **Per society.** Average the days late across invoices, **weighted by amount**, over a rolling 12 months. D&B's Days Beyond Terms works this way; its PAYDEX score treats 80 as "prompt", 70 as about 15 days late and 50 as about 30 days late ([D&B FAQ](https://docs.dnb.com/static/doc-uploads/supplier/en-US/support/FAQs.pdf)).
- **Unpaid invoices.** Count an invoice still unpaid at its current days overdue, growing each day, so that a non-payer never looks better than a late payer.
- **Disputes and old records.** Pause any invoice under dispute. Leave out invoices recorded as paid at intake that carry no real payment date.
- **Too little history.** Give a society with too little history no score at all, as CIBIL does with its MSME Rank ([TransUnion CIBIL](https://www.transunioncibil.com/press-release/transunion-cibil-launches-cibil-msme-rank)). Show "Not enough history" below about three released invoices.
- **Small samples.** Pull small-sample scores toward the portfolio average, and pick stars on the lower bound of the score rather than the raw average. Otherwise a society with two good months outranks one with twenty ([Evan Miller](https://evanmiller.org/how-not-to-sort-by-average-rating.html)).
- **Avoid flicker.** Make it harder to enter a list than to stay on it. Re-evaluate monthly, and expire every showcase entry after about 90 days so that it needs re-approval. Rating agencies change a grade only when a shift looks persistent ([NYU archive](https://archive.nyu.edu/handle/2451/26557)). The specific thresholds are design choices; no source sets them.

**Later parameters.** Store each parameter as a configuration row with its weight, its minimum data requirement and its decay. Show on every card which parameters contributed.

**Staff behaviour, if you record it at all.** Use dated incidents from a fixed list (refused entry, abusive language, obstructed work, helpful), with evidence. Count them only after two different executives report, and weigh positive and negative incidents equally. Raters are more consistent when they judge observable behaviour against anchored descriptions rather than general impressions ([IU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.indianapolis.iu.edu/items/8647cf95-224d-41c0-8715-f931f4d330bb)). This should support private conversations with a committee. Its subjectivity is exactly why it should never feed a published label.

### Approval that turns your existing flag into due process

**The defaulter flag.** Your existing flag (marked-at, marked-by and reason on the society) should remain internal, but its trigger should become factual. Two triggers fit:
- the arrears clock reaching "suspended";
- an invoice past a set number of days overdue, with payment status confirmed recently.

**The private sequence for a flagged society.** Base it on Uber's warnings and the RBI's notice-and-reply process:
1. The flag sends the society's office-bearer a dated notice in their own portal. It names the invoice, the amount and the due date, states the published rule, and gives at least 15 days to pay or dispute.
2. A dispute pauses the flag and goes to a reviewer other than whoever raised the flag.
3. The flag clears itself when the invoice is settled, and the clearing is logged.

This puts the strongest lever the evidence found — the known threat, delivered privately — at full strength. The listing rule should also appear in the agreement, on invoices and in reminders, so every society knows it exists. The UK result shows that a rule nobody knows about deters nobody.

**Star listings.** Use your existing date-change approval pattern: one person proposes, a different admin with a specific permission approves, nobody approves their own entry, and every action is audited. The approval screen should show:
- the rule that fired, in words;
- the invoices and payments behind it;
- the score breakdown;
- the template text;
- the expiry date;
- the committee's consent record.

Before approval goes through, the system should re-check that the facts still hold, and close the request as superseded if they do not, as your date-change requests already do.

### Words and screens

Name the positive list in ordinary words: "Societies that pay on time" or "Star partner societies". Template its text from facts, for example: "Paid all 12 invoices on or before the due date since July 2025." This follows the boomerang fix: recognise against a standard, with clear approval.

Never use "defaulter", "worst" or a bottom-of-table rank on any surface another society can see. In Indian banking, "defaulter" implies a formal classification that FirsThing does not perform. When a society sees its *own* overdue invoice, state the fact plainly: "Invoice FT/2026-27/055 of ₹16,579 was due on 15-08-2026 and is unpaid as of 21-10-2026." Never show committee members' names or photographs.

**Carousel.** An auto-rotating carousel must have a visible pause control under WCAG 2.2 SC 2.2.2, which is Level A. The W3C's pattern also pauses it on hover or keyboard focus and starts it paused when the device asks for reduced motion ([W3C APG](https://w3c.github.io/wai-website/ARIA/apg/patterns/carousel/examples/carousel-1-prev-next/)). Usability research reported by several universities found that people miss content in rotating carousels, mistake them for ads, and scroll past them on phones before the slide changes ([Yale usability](https://usability.yale.edu/usability-best-practices/hero-imagescarousels)). One study found a carousel was noticed as a signal of importance ([TecEd](https://teced.com/?p=3642)).

A static row of three star cards with "See all", which becomes a swipeable row on a phone, gets the attention without those costs.

**Stats strip.** Societies served, lights retrofitted, kWh saved this year, and the share of societies fully paid this month. The last figure carries the "most people pay on time" message without naming anyone. Each figure should carry an as-of date, and the strip should link only to the full star list.

## Conclusion

The idea rests on an assumption the evidence does not support: that seeing who is publicly named is what changes behaviour. What changes behaviour is the credible, specific prospect of a consequence, communicated privately and early, together with a clear signal that paying on time is normal and appreciated. A peer-visible defaulter wall adds legal exposure, and it is the part of the idea that the Kerala ruling, the defamation truth defence and the DPDP Act all bear on. It adds little payment beyond what the warning already secures, and it can fall on a committee that did not cause the lapse.

FirsThing already holds most of what the better design needs: invoice ageing, TDS-aware settlement, an arrears clock, an internal flag and a maker-checker approval flow. What it lacks is the private notice-and-reply step and an opt-in star showcase. Both are small to build. Before launch, have counsel check four things:
- the confidentiality and dispute clauses in the society agreements;
- whether FirsThing qualifies under the MSMED Act and a society counts as a buyer;
- the final text of the BNS exceptions;
- the consent wording for star listings.
