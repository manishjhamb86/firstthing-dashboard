# What makes a UI colour system read as "classic/premium" rather than "cheap," and how serious multi-theme design systems build a categorical colour set across light/dark/slate

## Key Question 1: What concrete design qualities (not vague adjectives) are named by credible sources as producing a "classic"/"premium" feel via colour choice specifically?

### Takeaway
No single named, high-authority source gives a rigorous theory of "premium via colour" with controlled evidence — most of the strongest, most specific, most *actionable* guidance comes from secondary/blog-tier sources (UX Planet, agency blogs) repeating the same handful of concrete mechanisms: (1) tint every neutral with a trace of a single brand/base hue rather than using pure gray/white/black, (2) hold saturation low across the bulk of the UI and spend high saturation only on a few interactive/brand moments, (3) never use literal `#000000`/`#FFFFFF`, and (4) build each colour as a 5–9-step tonal *scale*, not a single flat swatch. These are consistent, repeated, concrete claims — but confidence should be rated **medium**, because the clearest statements of them come from blog authors without institutional backing, not from a peer-reviewed or vendor-authoritative source. One genuinely strong, measured, named-study-backed claim did surface on the *saturation ↔ perceived price* link specifically (medium-high confidence, traces to a cited consumer-research study rather than just a blogger's assertion).

### Cited Findings
- **Tinted neutrals, not pure neutrals, is "the one color decision" argued to separate premium products from merely assembled ones.** A UX Planet piece argues explicitly that polished interfaces (it names Linear, Stripe, Vercel, and Raycast) "tint their surfaces, borders, and shadows with the brand hue rather than using pure white or pure black," and frames this as the specific, nameable decision that makes a UI "feel designed" vs. "feel assembled." — [UX Planet: "The One Color Decision That Makes a UI Look Expensive"](https://uxplanet.org/the-one-color-decision-that-makes-a-ui-look-expensive-d6890efe11ba) (medium confidence — single blog author, framed as opinion/argument, not data-backed; could not retrieve full text directly, a 403 blocked direct fetch, so this is from the search-snippet summary only)
- **Refactoring UI (Adam Wathan & Steve Schoger)** is repeatedly summarized (via third-party study-guides/skills, not the book itself) as advising: tint grays with a slight hue (blue for a "cool" UI, yellow/brown for "warm") rather than using neutral gray; never use pure black for the darkest shade — use a dark-gray like `#111827` instead; and build each color as a **5–9-step shade scale** (50 through 900) rather than a single flat value, adjusting HSL at the light and dark ends. — [Refactoring UI summary via Smithery/Playbooks skill pages](https://smithery.ai/skills/wondelai/refactoring-ui); [restated via a Claude Code skill reference](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/claude-code-skills@0.5.0/data/skills/ux-ui-design-system/references/refactoring-ui.md) (medium confidence — I could not access Wathan/Schoger's original book text directly; all citations here are third-party summaries/paraphrases of it, so exact wording is unverified, but the *substance* is repeated consistently across multiple independent summarizer sources, which raises confidence the underlying claims are real)
- **Saturation ↔ perceived price, with an actual cited study.** An Oprah.com piece on overspending cites a *Journal of Consumer Research* study in which "people paid more for a suitcase in a highly saturated color than in a muted one," and states the general finding that "we perceive products to be larger or smaller depending on the saturation of their color, and that perception influences how much we'll pay." — [Oprah.com, "How to Stop Overspending"](https://www.oprah.com/home/how-to-stop-overspending) (medium-high confidence for the *existence* of this consumer-research finding — it's attributed to a named academic journal, not just a blog assertion — but this is a secondary report of the study, not the primary paper itself, and the direction of the effect (does *more* saturation reliably mean "feels more expensive," or does it depend on category/context?) is not fully pinned down by this source alone)
- **A UX "saturation guide"** (uxcel.com / atmos.style-adjacent summary) states that "most practical UI palettes use relatively low saturation for backgrounds, text, and borders, and reserve high saturation for key interactive elements and brand expression," and warns that "overusing high saturation produces UIs that feel overwhelming and hard to scan." — [via WebSearch summary, uxcel.com "ux color" skill reference] (lower confidence — no single named author attributable, reads as general design-blog consensus)
- **Restraint over additive effects, in print design specifically** (adjacent domain, offered as a cross-medium parallel): Dayfold argues that "stacking foils, embossing, and other effects can make print look less premium," and that editing down to a single dominant idea reads as higher-end — i.e., restraint itself, not any specific colour, is the premium signal. — [Dayfold, "Why Overdesigned Print Often Feels Cheaper"](https://www.dayfold.com/?p=400) (medium confidence, named small studio, but print-domain not UI-domain; offered as an analogy, not direct evidence for screen UI)
- **Cognitive-ease framing** (lower confidence, no clearly named/credentialed author found): a general marketing-guide source claims clean/organized layouts are processed more easily by the brain and that this ease itself reads as "premium," independent of colour. — flagged explicitly as **opinion/marketing-guide tier, not a named design authority**; included only because it's a recurring claim worth being aware of, not because it's well-evidenced.

### Inferences
- The strongest, most *repeatable, implementable* rule across every source found is: **a palette that visibly shares one undertone across its neutrals (grays/backgrounds) reads as deliberate; a palette whose neutrals are pure/neutral-gray while its accents are saturated reads as "colour slapped on top of a generic UI kit."** This is the mechanism underlying the "tinted neutrals" claim and is the single most concrete, source-repeated answer to "what produces premium via colour."
- The "5–9 step scale per colour, never pure black/white" guidance (Refactoring UI) and the "restrained saturation on 90%+ of the UI, spent sparingly" guidance both point at the same underlying principle as the categorical-palette question in this brief: **premium ≈ consistency of restraint across every colour decision**, not any single hue choice. This generalizes directly to a 5-colour categorical system — if 4 of 5 category colours are desaturated/muted and one is left saturated, the deliberate-restraint signal breaks, and *that* specific inconsistency (not the "cheap" colour alone) is what likely reads as off.
- No source directly measured or claimed "classic"/"timeless" as a colour-specific property distinct from "premium" — treat these as functionally the same signal set in the sources available (restraint, tonal consistency, scale-based construction) rather than two separate design vocabularies.

### Gaps
- No primary-source access to Refactoring UI's actual book text was possible in this research pass (all citations are third-party summaries/paraphrases); the report-writer should treat specific numeric claims (e.g. "5–9 shades," the exact hex `#111827`) as *likely accurate paraphrases* but not verbatim-verified quotes.
- The UX Planet "one color decision" article could not be fetched directly (403 Forbidden) — only the search-engine snippet summary was available, so the full argument and any additional supporting detail beyond what's quoted above is unconfirmed.
- No controlled study was found that isolates "shared undertone across a palette" as a causal driver of perceived premium-ness specifically in *software UI* (as opposed to the suitcase/product-colour study, which is about a single product's colour, not a multi-hue dashboard system). This is a real evidence gap: the "tinted neutrals" and "shared temperature" claims are argued-by-example (naming Linear/Stripe/Vercel/Raycast) rather than tested.
- Found nothing from a named senior visual designer (e.g., a personal blog/talk by a known individual designer, as the brief specifically asked for) with first-person stated reasoning on "why does this look cheap" — only aggregator/blog-tier material surfaced in searches performed.

## Key Question 2: Is there documented colour-theory guidance (UI-specific) that a palette reading "corporate/premium/editorial" vs. "playful/consumer/cheap" comes from shared saturation/lightness bands across hues vs. mismatched bands — and any named "jewel tone"/"muted palette" guidance for software UI?

### Takeaway
Yes, directionally — multiple independent sources converge on **"consistent chroma/saturation + consistent lightness steps across every hue in the palette, with hue held essentially fixed at each step" as the mechanical definition of a palette that reads as deliberate/professional**, versus hues pulled from mismatched saturation/lightness bands reading as inconsistent/assembled. The strongest, most *technically precise* version of this claim comes from Evil Martians' Harmonizer tool documentation (medium-high confidence — named, credible frontend/design engineering studio, with a specific technical claim about why plain HSL fails at this). The "jewel tone" framing specifically is thin in the sources found — it exists as a marketing/mood-board concept (muted, saturated-but-dark hues reading as "rich"/"premium"/"calm") but was not found tied to a single authoritative named colour-theory source; treat jewel-tone-specific claims as **lower confidence**.

### Cited Findings
- **Evil Martians' Harmonizer** states its explicit design goal is palettes with **"perceptually consistent lightness and contrast"** across all levels and hues, built on the OKLCH colour model plus the APCA contrast algorithm, specifically because (their stated problem) "same 'level' colors vary in lightness/chroma" when built in ordinary ways, "contrast guesswork" forces hand-adjustment of text per background, and "light/dark modes require custom mappings." — [Evil Martians, Harmonizer](https://evilmartians.com/opensource/harmonizer) (high confidence — named, credible design-engineering studio explicitly building a tool to solve exactly this problem; this is the single most directly on-topic source found)
- **Why plain HSL fails at this (named, if secondary, explanation):** a UX Design (Medium) explainer on OKLCH states that "yellow and blue at the same [HSL] L value appear wildly different in actual brightness" because HSL's lightness channel is not perceptually uniform, whereas OKLCH's lightness channel is *designed* to be perceptually uniform, so "a 10-point L change looks the same regardless of hue." This is the concrete mechanical reason a palette assembled by picking "5 hues at the same HSL saturation/lightness" can still look mismatched in actual *apparent* weight even though the numbers match. — [UX Design (Medium), "OKLCH, explained for designers"](https://uxdesign.cc/oklch-explained-for-designers-dc6af4433611) (medium confidence — named publication, unverified named individual author in the search snippet, standard "how OKLCH differs from HSL" explainer content that is widely corroborated elsewhere)
- **A palette-construction guide** (found via Preline's theme-generator skill docs) gives a specific, numeric version of "consistent hue/chroma band = professional": **chroma should peak at mid-tones and stay very low at both extremes, with hue held to roughly ±5° across the scale**; "vibrant" brand colours sit around chroma 0.10–0.20 (OKLCH), while "muted/soft" variants drop to chroma 0.02–0.05. — [Preline theme-generator palette-guidance doc](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/preline@4.2.0/skills/theme-generator/docs/palette-guidance.md) (lower-medium confidence — a tool/library's internal guidance doc, not a named individual design authority, but it's a specific, falsifiable numeric claim rather than a vague adjective)
- **Saturation as the explicit professional/playful lever, with named examples:** the same search thread surfaced guidance that "high saturation [suits] playful, energetic brands" while "restrained saturation [suits] premium and enterprise products," and separately that "cool colours feel calm and professional, and most enterprise and SaaS interfaces lean cool to reduce visual fatigue during extended use," with triadic (3-hue, high-contrast) schemes recommended for "playful, category-heavy products" versus monochromatic/restrained schemes for the "safer," more premium-reading choice. — [via WebSearch summary of UX color-skill and palette guides] (lower confidence — general design-blog consensus, no single named authority pinned down for the specific corporate/playful framing)
- **Muted tones named explicitly as reading "modern, premium, and trustworthy," recommended as the base layer for dashboards/cards specifically, with saturated colour reserved as accent.** — [Flatirons, "Muted Colors"](https://flatirons.com/blog/muted-colors); [Wavespace Agency, "What Are Muted Colors?"](https://www.wavespace.agency/blog/muted-colors) (lower-medium confidence — named agencies, but content reads as marketing-blog tier rather than a rigorous colour-theory source; directionally consistent with every other source above, which raises aggregate confidence even though no single source here is individually strong)
- **Jewel-tone-specific guidance was thin.** The one closest match defines "tones" mechanically as "muted variations, created by reducing saturation" of a base jewel hue, and recommends pairing a jewel colour (e.g. jade) with "soft neutrals and slightly warm grays," while flagging that cooler jewel palettes "can feel more severe than expected" — i.e., a warning that a jewel-tone system still needs a *warm* undertone somewhere (in the neutrals) to avoid reading as cold/severe rather than rich/premium. — [instantgradient.com, Jade Jewel colour page] (lower confidence — a colour-reference/marketing site, not a named design authority; the "severe" warning is a useful, specific, actionable caution but is uncorroborated elsewhere in this research pass)

### Inferences
- Combining the Harmonizer/OKLCH finding with the Preline numeric guidance gives a **directly implementable rule for a 5-colour categorical system**: pick each category hue, then constrain all five to the *same* OKLCH lightness value (or a tight, deliberate band of 2–3 lightness steps used consistently for the same *role* — e.g. all "icon tint" instances at one L, all "accent border" instances at another L) and the *same* chroma band (e.g. all five in the 0.08–0.14 "muted-but-identifiable" chroma range, not one at 0.20 and another at 0.03). This is the mechanical translation of "shared saturation/lightness band across hues = professional" into something buildable in CSS today (OKLCH is supported Chrome 111+/Firefox 113+/Safari 15.4+, per the earlier OKLCH-dark-mode search).
- The "jewel tone" framing is best understood, from the available sources, as a *subset* of "muted but not desaturated-to-gray" — i.e., jewel tones are saturated-but-dark/deep hues (think emerald, sapphire, garnet: chroma present, lightness low-to-mid), which is actually the *opposite* lightness direction from "pastel/muted" in the pale sense. A palette built from jewel tones gets its "premium" read from *depth* (lower lightness, moderate-to-high chroma) rather than from *desaturation* (low chroma, often higher lightness) — these are two different techniques both labeled "muted" colloquially but mechanically distinct, and a report-writer should not conflate them. No single source cleanly separated this distinction; it's inferred from reading the jewel-tone and muted-palette sources against each other.
- "Cool hues read as professional/enterprise" is a recurring but unattributed claim; it should be treated as **directional folk-wisdom with some corroboration**, not as settled colour theory — a jewel-tone, multi-hue categorical system (which by definition spans warm and cool hues, since you need 5 distinguishable categories) cannot rely on "stay in the cool range" as its premium mechanism; it has to rely on the shared-lightness/shared-chroma-band mechanism instead, which is hue-agnostic.

### Gaps
- Did not find a single, individually-authoritative, named colour-theorist source making the specific "shared saturation/lightness band = corporate, mismatched bands = cheap" claim with evidence (as opposed to the Harmonizer tool's *implicit* endorsement of this via its stated design goals). This claim is well-triangulated across several lower-confidence sources but not confirmed by one strong one.
- Could not find the Refactoring UI book's own direct colour-theory content on this specific point (corporate vs. playful via saturation bands) beyond the general "keep saturation low for most of the UI" guidance already covered in Key Question 1 — the book may address this more precisely in its original text, which was not accessible in this research pass.
- "Jewel tone" as a named, rigorous UI-design-system term (as opposed to a general colour-naming/fashion term) was not found documented by any of the specific systems requested (Radix, Carbon, Polaris, Atlassian, Material) — none of them use "jewel tone" as internal vocabulary in the material retrieved.

## Key Question 3: How do serious multi-theme design systems (Radix Colors, Material Design 3, Polaris, Carbon) construct a SET of categorical colours that each independently look correct and equally weighted in both light and dark themes — what is the documented METHOD for deriving a dark-mode version of a light-mode hue?

### Takeaway
Of the four systems requested, **Radix Colors** is the one built explicitly and rigorously for this exact problem (a scale-per-hue, each with a light and dark version, each step given a fixed *role* rather than a fixed look), but its own site documentation, as retrieved in this pass, states the *contract* (steps 1–12, roles, APCA-based guaranteed contrast) far more clearly than it states the *derivation method* for how dark-mode hues are picked to still "feel like the same hue family" — this was not found spelled out in prose on the pages retrieved. **Carbon** has the most explicit, documented stance of the four on categorical-colour *order* and separation from status colour, but its dark-theme categorical values were only found via a third-party package, not Carbon's own docs. **Material Design 3**'s tonal-palette/HCT system is the most mathematically rigorous of the four (generating palettes in HCT space, with light/dark themes using *different tone numbers* from the same underlying hue/chroma palette) but direct access to Google's own prose explanation failed in this pass; findings here are from third-party summarizers and should be verified against m3.material.io directly. **Polaris**/Shopify's relevant system (Polaris Viz) was reportedly **archived in June 2025** per a third-party note — treat as a historical rather than current reference.

### Cited Findings
- **Radix Colors — the step-role contract (confirmed from Radix's own site):** "Each step is designed with a specific use case in mind, such as backgrounds, hover states, borders, overlays, or text." Steps are grouped: ~1–2 for app/subtle backgrounds, 3–5 for interactive component states, 6–8 for borders/separators, **9 for solid/brand backgrounds (most saturated step, "least mix of white or black," designed to sit under white text)**, 10 for hover/active solid states, and **11–12 for accessible text** (11 = low-contrast text, 12 = high-contrast text). — [radix-ui.com/colors](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors) (high confidence, retrieved directly from Radix's own site); step-9/10/11 role detail corroborated by a Zed editor source-code comment and third-party docs ([pub.dev radix_colors docs](https://pub.dev/documentation/radix_colors/latest/); Zed source via Codeberg) (medium confidence for the step-9/10 detail, since it's corroborated by a code comment rather than Radix's own prose)
- **Radix Colors — the dark-mode mechanism, as *stated*, is switch-based, not derivation-explained:** Radix's own docs state only that "Switching to dark theme is as simple as applying a class to a container" — i.e., each scale ships as a *pre-built pair* (light scale + a separately-authored, same-named dark scale), and the *site prose retrieved did not explain the authoring method* used to pick the dark scale's actual colour values. — [radix-ui.com/colors](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors) (high confidence that this is what the page says; explicitly **not** a confident source for *how* the dark values were chosen — that reasoning was not found in this pass)
- **Radix Colors — the contrast guarantee that constrains the dark-mode derivation:** "Text colors are guaranteed to pass target contrast ratios against the corresponding background colors," with contrast targets based on the **APCA** algorithm (not WCAG's older ratio formula) — this is the one concrete, falsifiable design constraint found that the dark-mode scales must satisfy, even though the hue-selection method itself wasn't found in prose. — [radix-ui.com/colors](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors) (high confidence, direct retrieval)
- **Material Design 3 — tonal palette mechanism (third-party summary, unverified against Google's own prose in this pass):** M3 generates palettes from a single seed colour in **HCT space** (Hue, Chroma, Tone), producing tones 0–100 for each of several roles (primary, secondary, tertiary — tertiary's hue shifted ~60° from the seed). The claimed **dark-mode mechanism is tone *inversion*, not hue change**: light themes use *low-tone* backgrounds with *high-tone* content (e.g., tone-99 surface / tone-10 text), while dark themes invert which tones serve as surface vs. content (e.g., tone-80 content on a tone-10/20 background) — the *hue and chroma stay anchored to the same seed-derived palette*; only which tone number is assigned to "background" vs. "text/accent" flips between themes. A Cordova plugin's API docs independently confirm this inversion framing: "in dark themes, the usage is inverted: higher tonal values for text and lower values for backgrounds." — [third-party M3 guide, via search]; [Cordova dynamic-color plugin docs via Mintlify](https://www.mintlify.com/ollm/cordova-plugin-dynamic-color/api/palette) (medium confidence — corroborated by two independent third-party sources describing the same tone-inversion mechanism, but **not verified against Google's own m3.material.io prose**, which could not be successfully fetched in this pass — direct fetch returned only a page title with no body content)
- **Carbon — categorical (qualitative) palette is explicitly separate from status/alert colour, with a stated ordering rationale:** Carbon's own data-visualization docs state the 14-colour categorical sequence (Purple 70, Cyan 50, Teal 70, Magenta 70, Red 50, Red 90, …) "should be applied in sequence strictly as described," because "the sequence is carefully curated to maximize contrast between neighboring colors to help with visual differentiation" — i.e., the *order itself*, not just the individual hues, is a designed artifact meant to prevent adjacent categories from being confused. Carbon maintains a **wholly separate "Alert" palette** for status (red=danger/error, orange=serious warning, yellow=regular warning, green=normal/success), explicitly distinct from the categorical/chart palette. — [carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes](https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/) (high confidence — direct retrieval from Carbon's own docs for the categorical-sequence and separate-alert-palette claims)
- **Carbon — dark-theme categorical values (third-party source, not Carbon's own docs):** an npm package built on `@carbon/charts` lists dark-mode categorical values for the first six categories as Purple 60, Cyan 40, Teal 60, Magenta 40, Red 50, Red 10 — i.e., the **dark-mode hue is a different numbered *step* on Carbon's own underlying 10/100-scale colour ramp for the same named hue family** (e.g. "Purple" moves from step 70 in light mode to step 60 in dark mode), rather than an unrelated colour substitution. — [@polyxd/ds-carbon npm package](https://socket.dev/npm/package/@polyxd/ds-carbon) (lower-medium confidence — a third-party package, explicitly flagged by the searcher as needing verification against the actual `@carbon/charts` source; Carbon's own retrieved docs did not state these dark values directly)
- **Carbon — monochromatic/sequential (not categorical) dark-mode rule, stated directly:** "For sequential scales, in dark themes, the lightest color denotes the largest values" (the inverse of light-mode's darkest-color-means-largest convention) — this is a *different* palette type from categorical, but shows Carbon applies the same "flip which end of the scale reads as 'most'" logic for sequential data that M3 applies via tone-inversion for surfaces/content. — [carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes](https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/) (high confidence, direct retrieval)
- **Polaris (Shopify) — archived status and prior method:** Shopify's engineering blog states Polaris Viz was built so "admin charts [stay] consistent across teams" with "built-in light and dark themes," replacing per-chart custom styling with a shared `theme` prop. A Shopify Insights designer (Miru Alves) reportedly built a 7-colour palette "using Delta-E and contrast-ratio calculations" specifically to avoid accessibility violations across the set. Third-party summaries (unverified against Shopify's own docs) describe category rules: single series = one consistent colour; comparisons-to-past use purple for "current" and grey for "historical"; positive/negative deltas use green/red; max 4 lines per multi-series chart. **A third-party note states Polaris Viz was archived in June 2025**, with Shopify reportedly directing teams to contact support for migration — this status was not independently confirmed against Shopify's own current documentation. — [Shopify Engineering, "A React library for consistent data visualization"](https://shopify.engineering/react-library-consistent-data-visualization); archival status via third-party summary only (medium confidence for the historical design method; **low confidence / unverified** for the archival claim — flagged explicitly since it affects whether Polaris should be cited as a *current* reference at all)

### Inferences
- Across all three systems with clear documentation (Radix, Material, Carbon), the **common underlying method, even though none of the retrieved pages spelled it out as a single unified "how-to," is: anchor each hue to a fixed position in an underlying perceptually-structured scale (Radix's named steps, Material's HCT tones, Carbon's 10/100 ramp step numbers), and let dark mode reuse the *same scale*, just selecting different step numbers/tones for the same semantic *role*.** Nobody in this research set is picking dark-mode colours by eye, hue-by-hue; everyone is picking them by *position in a pre-built, shared ramp*. This is the most important, generalizable, cross-source-corroborated finding of this research pass and should be the headline method recommendation: **build (or adopt) a 10–12-step lightness/chroma ramp per hue, assign your five categories fixed step-numbers for each theme (e.g. "icon tint = step 9 in light, step 10 in dark" per hue), rather than hand-picking five dark-mode hex values independently.**
- The Radix "step 9 is the single most saturated step, designed for white text on top" convention is directly applicable to an icon-tint/card-accent-border/badge system: it implies the *badge background* role and the *icon tint* role are naturally *different* steps (badge-as-solid-fill wants step-9-equivalent; a subtle accent border or a muted icon tint wants something lower, e.g. step 6–8-equivalent), which is a concrete way to keep "icon tint," "card border," and "badge" visually related (same hue, same scale) without being visually identical (different steps = different weights for different jobs) — exactly the "equally weighted across five hues, but differentiated by role" requirement in the brief.
- Carbon's explicit "the *order* of categorical colours is itself designed to maximize adjacent-category contrast" is a transferable technique even for a fixed 5-category (not Carbon's 14) system: don't just pick 5 individually-nice hues, check the hues *as ordered/juxtaposed* (e.g. on a legend or in adjacent rows) for confusability, the same way Carbon sequenced Purple→Cyan→Teal→Magenta→Red specifically to keep neighbours distinct.

### Gaps
- Material Design 3's own prose on m3.material.io could not be retrieved in this pass (the fetch returned only a bare page title, likely a JS-rendered page that didn't resolve to readable content via the fetch tool) — all MD3 findings above are **third-party summaries and should be directly verified against m3.material.io/styles/color/system/how-the-system-works and the `material-color-utilities` source** before being treated as Google's own stated rationale rather than a paraphrase.
- Radix Colors' own blog/announcement posts (the brief specifically asked for "its own documentation/blog posts about why it's built the way it is") were searched for directly (including by author name, Colm Tuite / Vlad Moroz) and not found in this pass — only the acquisition-announcement blog post (Modulz→WorkOS) surfaced, which contains no colour-system rationale. The GitHub repository (github.com/radix-ui/colors) was identified as the likely location for this but was not fetched in this pass.
- No source found gave a *numerically explicit* worked example of "here is light-mode hue X at OKLCH (L, C, H); here is its dark-mode counterpart at OKLCH (L', C, H) and here is why L' and C' were chosen" for any of the four named systems specifically. The closest is the general OKLCH-dark-mode search result describing "invert lightness values... keep colors harmonious" as a general technique (colorbox.io), not tied to any of the four requested systems.
- Polaris/Shopify's current (2026) status and documentation were not independently confirmed; if Polaris Viz is genuinely archived, it may be worth excluding from an "active, current" systems citation unless the report-writer independently re-verifies this.

## Key Question 4: Is there documented guidance on reusing existing semantic (status) tones to ALSO mean "category" elsewhere in the same product, vs. the recommendation to always keep semantic and categorical palettes entirely separate?

### Takeaway
The clearest, most consistent guidance found is **"keep status and category separate — a colour's meaning must be fixed and singular across the product, or users relearn/misread it under the exact conditions (errors, urgency) when correct reading matters most."** This is stated plainly by a community design-system skill reference and is structurally confirmed by how Carbon maintains a wholly separate Alert palette from its categorical/chart palette. However, one genuinely named, credible enterprise design system — **Salt (Citi's design system)** — was found to *structurally* overlap its "status" and "sentiment" palettes (Negative=red=Error, Positive=green=Success, Caution=orange=Warning), **without ever stating this overlap is a problem or defending it as intentional** — which is itself an important, citable data point: even a serious financial-industry design system did not fully achieve "zero semantic/categorical hue reuse" in practice, and didn't document why. Treat the "always separate" recommendation as the documented *ideal/best-practice*, while noting the "sentiment" case shows real systems blur this specifically when the second palette (sentiment) is *itself* meaning-carrying (not neutral/arbitrary labeling) — which is a different situation from a purely arbitrary "category/domain" palette like the one in this brief.

### Cited Findings
- **"Too many semantic colours confuse users; each colour should map to exactly one meaning," with an explicit warning about cross-component drift:** a community design-system reference states "users must learn what each colour means, remember it, and interpret it correctly under stress, which is when errors tend to occur," and explicitly warns: **"if orange means 'warning' in one component and 'pending' in another, the system breaks down."** It recommends a small, fixed standard set (red=error/destructive, orange=warning, green=success, blue=informational) and explicitly suggests **neutral grey** as the fallback "when unsure, since grey conveys status without carrying semantic weight." — [via WebSearch summary of a "status-colors-and-errors" Claude Code skill reference (dembrandt)] (lower-medium confidence — a community-authored reference/skill doc, not an institutional design-system authority, but the "orange=warning in one place, pending in another" failure mode it names is a concrete, specific, falsifiable risk statement rather than a vague platitude, which raises its practical value even at modest source-authority)
- **Salt Design System (Citi) distinguishes "status" colours (communicate current system/process state: Error=red, Info=blue, Success=green, Warning/Caution=orange) from "sentiment" colours (guide users, shape how they feel: Accent=teal/trust, Caution=orange/carefulness, Negative=red/negativity, Neutral=gray/neutrality, Positive=green/positivity) as two separate documented token families** — but on direct inspection, **the actual hue assignments overlap one-to-one**: Caution (sentiment) uses the same orange as Warning (status); Negative (sentiment) uses the same red as Error (status); Positive (sentiment) uses the same green as Success (status). The page states tokens are organized "by their intended meaning" for consistency, but **does not state whether this overlap is deliberate, does not flag it as a tension, and does not explain how a reader is meant to tell "this red means an error occurred" apart from "this red means this value/outcome is negative."** — [Salt Design System, "Semantic use of color"](https://www.saltdesignsystem.com/salt/foundations/color/semantic-use-of-color) (high confidence that this is literally what the page documents — direct retrieval; but this is evidence of **practice**, not of a stated **recommendation** — Salt doesn't argue for or defend the overlap, it simply has it)
- **Atlassian's token system is role-based (not hue-based) specifically to prevent this confusion, and explicitly instructs against layering an *additional* decorative meaning onto an already-semantic colour:** Atlassian's design-tokens documentation states "Don't use an accent when the color already has semantic meaning," and structures tokens so a raw hue (e.g. "Purple800," a base/primitive token) is *aliased* to a semantic token (e.g. `color.text.discovery`) — keeping the *palette* (primitives) and the *meaning-assignment* (semantic aliases) as two separate layers, so a primitive colour can in principle be reused across contexts, but a given *semantic* token's meaning stays fixed. — [Atlassian Design System, tokens documentation, via cdn.jsdelivr.net mirror](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@atlaskit/tokens@13.0.4/tokens.docs.tsx) (medium confidence — direct textual match retrieved via a package-mirror rather than atlassian.design's live page, but the quoted instruction is specific and directly on-topic)
- **Carbon keeps categorical/chart colour and status/alert colour as two entirely separate, non-overlapping documented palettes** (14-colour qualitative sequence for charts; a 4-colour red/orange/yellow/green Alert palette for status) — found no instance in Carbon's retrieved docs of a chart category reusing one of the four Alert hues, though the categorical set *does* include a red and a green among its 14 (Red 50/Red 90 appear in the categorical sequence), which — while not explicitly flagged by Carbon's own docs — is a structural risk of exactly the kind Atlassian and the community skill reference warn about: a chart category could, in principle, be misread as "this is the error/success series" purely because it happens to be red/green, even though Carbon's intent for that category slot is purely "category 5 of 14," not status. — [carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes](https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/) (high confidence on what's documented; the risk-flagging is this researcher's own observation of the data, not a claim Carbon's docs make themselves — stated as an inference, see below)

### Inferences
- The practical, synthesizable rule from everything found is: **the "always separate" recommendation is strongest and most clearly defended when the second palette (categorical/tag) is genuinely arbitrary/neutral in meaning (e.g. "Deals," "Societies," "Lighting," "Water," "Billing" — domain labels with no inherent valence).** The moment a secondary palette *itself* carries valence/meaning (Salt's "sentiment": positive/negative/caution), the sources show real systems *do* let it share hues with true status colour, apparently on the reasoning that "negative sentiment" and "error status" are close enough in meaning that reuse doesn't create the Atlassian/community-skill-warned confusion — they're both "red = bad," just at different granularities (a value being negative vs. a process having failed). **For a purely domain/category system with no valence (the dashboard described in this brief), there is no such "close enough in meaning" justification available, so the stronger, better-defended "keep fully separate" rule should apply without the Salt-style exception.**
- Given Carbon's own categorical sequence includes red and green among 14 neutral-meaning categories, and no source defends this as deliberate, this should be read as a **documented absence of a clean answer** rather than evidence that reuse is fine: even a mature, widely-adopted system (Carbon/IBM) has this latent small risk in its qualitative palette, uncommented-on, which argues *for* deliberately avoiding red/green (the two hues almost universally status-coded as error/success across virtually every source and system touched in this research, including Carbon's own Alert palette, Salt's status palette, and the community skill reference's recommended default set) in a new categorical system — even though no source makes this recommendation explicitly for the categorical case, it is the clear, convergent implication of every semantic-colour source found.
- No source in this research pass stated a *quantitative* guideline (e.g., "categorical colours may be X degrees of hue away from your status reds/greens/ambers and still be considered 'safe'"). This is a genuine gap; the only actionable rule extractable is qualitative — avoid the specific hues already carrying fixed status meaning in the product, full stop, rather than a formula for "how close is too close."

### Gaps
- Did not find a single source that states, as an explicit, defended design-system rule, "you may reuse up to N of your status hues as category colours as long as [condition]" — every source found was either firmly on the "keep separate" side (Atlassian, the community skill reference) or showed unexplained overlap in practice (Salt) without arguing for it. The report-writer should present this as "practice varies, but the documented/defended guidance is uniformly on the separate side" rather than citing a source for a middle-ground rule, because none was found.
- Could not verify directly from Atlassian's live atlassian.design site (only a jsdelivr package mirror of its tokens file was successfully retrieved); the live Atlassian Design System "Lozenge"/"Tag" component documentation specifically (which would be the most directly on-topic page for "arbitrary category colour" guidance, as distinct from the general token-architecture page retrieved) was not found/fetched in this pass.
- Found no discussion in any source of how this semantic/categorical separation question interacts specifically with a **third, "slate" theme** (as opposed to a conventional light/dark pair) — none of the systems researched (Radix, Material, Carbon, Polaris, Atlassian, Salt) were found to document a three-theme (light/dark/slate-navy) categorical colour approach; all documented guidance found is for the conventional light/dark binary, and extending it to a third theme (ensuring the same five category hues remain mutually distinguishable and equally weighted against slate's specific navy-chrome ground, not just against pure white or pure black) is not directly covered by any source in this research pass. This is likely the single largest gap for the report-writer to flag back to the engineer: **slate-specific guidance does not appear to exist in the documented literature; it will have to be derived by applying the light/dark method (fixed step/tone positions on a shared per-hue ramp) a third time, picking the step/tone that reads correctly against slate's specific ground colour, rather than found as existing prescriptive guidance.**
