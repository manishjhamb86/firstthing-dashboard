# Star and defaulter society showcase — behavioural evidence on naming bad payers and recognising good ones

Scope: whether publicly naming defaulters and recognising good payers changes behaviour, the side effects, and the design choices that change the outcome. Applied to FirsThing's plan to show "star" and "defaulter" societies to committee members of all client societies.

Evidence quality markers used throughout:
- **[RCT]** — randomised field experiment.
- **[NE]** — natural experiment or quasi-experiment.
- **[Obs]** — observational study or evaluation.
- **[Weak]** — survey, anecdote or commentary.

## 1. Does naming and shaming debtors / tax delinquents work? Effect sizes, who responds, who does not

### Takeaway
Naming works, but modestly and selectively. Three patterns recur:
- **The threat does most of the work.** The threat or salience of being named moves payment; the act of publication adds little beyond it.
- **Effects concentrate where paying is easy.** They show up in small debts and in firms that care about reputation.
- **Large or entrenched debtors barely respond.** Where awareness is low (UK HMRC), naming does nothing measurable. Plain reminders and deterrence messages are at least as effective, at lower risk.

### Cited Findings
- **[RCT] Perez-Truglia & Troiano, US tax delinquents.** Letters went to about 34,000 delinquents in Kansas, Kentucky and Wisconsin who together owed about $500m. The letters cross-randomised the salience of financial penalties, shaming penalties (listing on a public delinquent list visible to neighbours) and peer comparisons — [NBER w21264](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21264).
  - **Small debts only.** Making delinquency status more visible increased compliance among people owing under $2,500, with no significant effect on larger debts. For debts up to $2,500 the shaming letter raised the probability of leaving the list by about 20% (relative) — [MPR News summary of authors' NYT piece](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2015/04/15/should-we-publicly-shame-tax-delinquents).
  - **Version conflict.** The NBER page's later summary says financial reminders raised payment regardless of debt size, and that information about neighbours' delinquency (the peer comparison) had no effect — [NBER w21264](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21264). Treat the "20%, under $2,500" figure as coming from earlier drafts and press. The robust points across versions are: (a) penalty salience helps; (b) peer comparison does not; (c) any shaming effect sits in small debts.
- **[NE] Dwenger & Treber, Slovenia ("Shaming for Tax Enforcement", Management Science 2022).** Slovenia announced a naming-and-shaming list four months before publishing it, which lets the study separate the threat from the act — [CEPR DP13194](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP13194.pdf); [VoxEU column](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/shaming-and-tax-compliance).
  - **The threat:** corporations cut tax debt by about 8.5%, and individuals by about 5%. The effect was strongest in industries where reputation matters.
  - **Publication itself:** the first list produced a further reduction among those shamed, but it was "marginal in terms of revenue and tapers off quickly". Already-compliant taxpayers stayed compliant.
- **[NE] Slemrod, Rehman & Waseem, Pakistan (REStat 2022).** Pakistan began publishing every taxpayer's income tax paid in 2012, and at the same time began honouring the top 100 payers per category — [NBER w25623](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25623).
  - Disclosure raised taxes paid by about 9 log points.
  - The recognition programme raised them by about 17 log points — [CEPR DP14463](https://cepr.org/publications/dp14463).
  - The authors link part of the effect to a shift in social norms.
- **[NE] Bø, Slemrod & Thoresen, Norway (AEJ: Policy 2015).** In 2001 Norway's tax lists moved online, making disclosure much more salient in some municipalities than in others.
  - Reported income among business owners rose about 3% where exposure rose most — [SSB DP 770](https://www.ssb.no/en/forskning/discussion-papers/taxes-on-the-internet); [CESifo WP 4107](https://www.cesifo.org/en/publications/2013/working-paper/taxes-internet-deterrence-effects-public-disclosure).
  - Commentary puts the total revenue effect at about 0.2% and calls it "one study, one country" — [Conversable Economist](https://conversableeconomist.com/2015/03/30/should-individual-tax-returns-be-public-information/).
- **[Obs] UK HMRC, publishing details of deliberate defaulters.** HMRC's own 2018 evaluation (by Quadrangle) found weak evidence of deterrence, mainly because awareness was low — [GOV.UK research page](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/evaluating-the-effectiveness-of-publishing-details-of-deliberate-defaulters); [Tax Journal](https://taxjournal.com/articles/publishing-deliberate-defaulters-not-effective-deterrent-25072018).
  - Only 28% of people knew of the publication, against 70% who knew of fines.
  - Some of those named did not know they had been named. Others felt anger or embarrassment, but none reported material effects. Financial penalties had the greater impact.
  - The scheme covers defaults of £25,000 or more, with names kept online for up to 12 months — [International Adviser](https://international-adviser.com/naming-and-shaming-tax-avoiders-doesnt-work).
- **[RCT meta-analysis] Antinyan & Asatryan, "Nudging for Tax Compliance" (Economic Journal).** About 1,000 effects from 45 RCTs — [ZEW DP](https://www.zew.de/PU86278).
  - Plain reminders raise compliance about 2.7 percentage points from a baseline of about 25%.
  - Morale/social-norm nudges add about 1.4 points; deterrence nudges add about 3.2 points.
  - An earlier version found morale nudges "on average ineffective" against neutral letters — [ZEW dp19055](https://ftp.zew.de/pub/zew-docs/dp/dp19055.pdf).
  - Nudges work better on late payers and in person. The authors flag possible selective reporting.
- **[Weak] India, income tax.** Since 2015 the Income Tax Department has published names of large defaulters (for example 18 entities owing over ₹500 crore in March 2015; at least 96 named by May 2017) — [Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/story/income-tax-department-publishes-names-of-18-tax-defaulters/1/217516.html); [Moneylife](https://www.moneylife.in/article/it-department-publishes-names-of-big-tax-defaulters/41076.html).
  - The stated purpose was partly to trace untraceable defaulters through public tips.
  - A show-cause notice came before publication, i.e. a private warning step.
  - No measured recovery data was found.
- **[Weak] UK duty to report payment practices** (large firms, from 2017). It was designed to create reputational pressure on slow payers, but no causal evaluation was found. ICAEW notes the absence of a centralised dataset that would allow one — [ICAEW 2023](https://www.icaew.com/insights/viewpoints-on-the-news/2023/may-2023/why-late-payments-need-a-labelling-rethink-and-stronger-data); [BEIS government response](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/574312/duty-to-report-on-payment-practices-and-performance-government-response.pdf).

### Inferences
- For FirsThing, **who would respond** is the society that is late through drift: a small overdue amount and an administrative lapse. A society in a genuine dispute or financial difficulty is the least likely to move. This mirrors the "small debts only" and "dirty plants only" heterogeneity found elsewhere.
- **Most of the effect comes before anyone is named.** The Slovenia threat effect dwarfs the publication effect. This argues for making the *possibility* of listing known (in the agreement, on invoices, in reminders) rather than relying on the list itself.
- **A list nobody notices does nothing** (the HMRC lesson). Conversely, a list that is very visible inside a small, peer-connected audience (committee members of client societies) raises both the power and the risk.

### Gaps
- The final published Perez-Truglia & Troiano tables (exact effect sizes by debt band) could not be retrieved; the NBER and press versions conflict.
- No causal evidence was found on India GST defaulter naming, Australia's Payment Times Reporting Scheme, or New Zealand.
- No study was found on naming *commercial customers* by their own supplier. All the naming evidence comes from a state or regulator naming taxpayers or polluters.

## 2. Social norms and social comparison nudges: framing, the boomerang effect, descriptive vs injunctive norms

### Takeaway
- **Descriptive norms work and cost almost nothing.** Telling a late payer that "most people pay on time" reliably raises payment.
- **They can backfire.** A descriptive comparison alone pulls above-norm performers *down* (the boomerang). Adding an injunctive signal of approval (a smiley) prevents this.
- **Peer comparisons alone are unreliable for debt.**
- **Effects decay and need repetition.**

### Cited Findings
- **[RCT] Hallsworth, List, Metcalfe & Vlaev, HMRC (J. Public Econ 2017).** More than 200,000 UK taxpayers took part; 100,000 in the first trial — [NBER w20007](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20007); [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/aug14/w20007.html).
  - Norm messages ("Nine out of ten people pay their tax on time"; a UK-specific version; and a minority version adding that the recipient is in the small minority who have not paid) raised payment of overdue tax at no cost.
  - Loss framing did not beat gain framing.
  - Public-good messages also helped.
- **[RCT] Schultz, Nolan, Cialdini, Goldstein & Griskevicius, Psychological Science 2007.** About 290 households received feedback on their usage against the neighbourhood average — [Bit by Bit summary](https://bitbybitbook.com/en/1st-ed/running-experiments/beyond-simple); [Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/languages/es-es/intervention/how-social-norms-messaging-reduced-energy-usage-by-up-to-1.72-kwhday); [Schultz et al. 2018 reprise](https://www.scranton.edu/faculty/nolan/pdfs/social-norms-reprise_perspectives_schultz-et-al2018.pdf).
  - With the descriptive norm alone, low users *increased* consumption: the boomerang.
  - Adding a smiley for below-average users and a frown for above-average users stopped the boomerang. Above-average users cut about 1.72 kWh/day, roughly 40% more than with the descriptive norm alone.
- **[RCT] Allcott & Rogers, Opower (AER 2014).** Six million households — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v104y2014i10p3003-37.html); [NBER w18492](https://www.nber.org/papers/18492).
  - Each report triggered "action and backsliding": effort decayed within two weeks of a report.
  - Response persisted over two years of repeated reports.
  - After the reports stopped, effects decayed by 10–20% a year.
  - The headline average effect size was not retrieved in this pass.
- **[RCT] Perez-Truglia & Troiano.** Telling debtors about neighbours' delinquency had no effect on payment — [NBER w21264](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21264).

### Inferences
- **A "star societies" section is the portal analogue of a descriptive norm.** It tells late payers that on-time payment is normal. That is the cheap, well-evidenced part.
- **The boomerang risk is real for the stars.** A punctual society that sees it sits comfortably above the bar may relax. Pairing recognition with clear approval and a standard ("paid within terms every month") is the evidenced fix.
- **The strongest evidenced lever is the private message to the late payer,** e.g. "N of M societies paid on time this month — yours is one of the few still outstanding." It outperforms public peer listing in the tax trials.

### Gaps
- No RCT was found that compares a *private* norm letter with a *public* list in the same population.
- No norm-messaging trial among Indian housing societies or Indian B2B payers was found.

## 3. Positive recognition vs public shaming — and leaderboards at the bottom

### Takeaway
In the best natural experiment (Pakistan), recognition did roughly twice as much as disclosure. But recognition only helps when it is visible appreciation without a rank:
- adding a peer ranking lowered performance, mostly among low performers;
- awards that signal "this behaviour is exceptional" can reduce it.

### Cited Findings
- **[NE] Pakistan.** The top-100 recognition programme had about 17 log points of effect, against about 9 for public disclosure — [CEPR DP14463](https://cepr.org/publications/dp14463).
- **[RCT] Ashraf, Bandiera & Lee, "Awards Unbundled" (JEBO 2014).** A nationwide training programme for Zambian health workers — [STICERD](https://sticerd.lse.ac.uk/_new/publications/abstract?index=4302); [J-PAL PDF](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/publications/Awards-unbundle_Science_January2014.pdf).
  - Employer recognition and social visibility increased performance.
  - Social comparison (rank against peers) *reduced* it, especially among low-ability trainees, who withheld effort to avoid learning their relative standing.
  - The effects persisted through the training.
- **[RCT] Robinson, Gallus, Lee & Rogers, "The Demotivating Effect (and Unintended Message) of Awards" (OBHDP 2021).** 15,329 students in 14 California districts — [HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/demotivating-effect-and-unintended-message-awards-0); [UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/retrospective-awards/).
  - Pre-announced attendance awards had no effect.
  - Surprise awards *reduced* later attendance, most among low performers.
  - Proposed mechanisms: the award signalled that the behaviour was neither the norm nor expected, and it licensed recipients to slack.

### Inferences
- **Recognise against a standard, not by rank.** For example: "paid within terms 12 months running". Avoid "Top 5" or "#1 society" rankings, which create a visible bottom.
- **Ranking societies on payment and staff behaviour** risks the Ashraf et al. pattern: those at the bottom disengage rather than improve.
- **Recognition must not imply rarity.** If only a few societies are stars, it signals that paying on time is unusual, which is the Robinson et al. mechanism. Show that most pay on time.

### Gaps
- No study was found on leaderboards in a B2B supplier–customer setting.
- No direct comparison was found of recognition-only vs recognition-plus-shaming in a payments context.

## 4. Relationship and commercial effects in B2B / services: churn, word of mouth, retaliation, brand damage

### Takeaway
Rigorous evidence on a supplier publicly shaming its own customers was not found; this is the biggest evidence gap. The adjacent evidence points to brand risk:
- consumers penalise brands seen as aggressive towards customers;
- a grievance aired in public is harder to repair than a private one.

### Cited Findings
- **[RCT/experimental, adjacent] Brand attitude.** Consumers' attitude to a brand fell when it threatened legal action against a consumer who created an ad for it — [summarised in search; Journal of Public Affairs study, via Bath research portal listing](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/when-companies-get-caught-the-effect-of-consumers-discovering-und/). The exact study details were not verified.
- **[Obs] Public complaints (Journal of Business Research, 2022).** Service recovery appeased customers who complained privately more than customers who had gone public — [eMarketer/Bath search summary](https://www.emarketer.com/content/shoppers-go-public-over-bad-service--not-bad-shipping). This came from a search summary; the primary source was not verified.
- **[Weak] Consumer surveys.**
  - 36% of social-media users say they would publicly shame a company that ignores them — [Sprout Social](https://sproutsocial.com/insights/press/thirty-six-percent-consumers-ready-shame-social/).
  - An Australian survey: "100%" would join in shaming a misbehaving industry — [B&T](https://www.bandt.com.au/?p=1171205).

### Inferences
- **A defaulter listing is published unilaterally by the creditor,** not by a neutral authority (unlike HMRC, Slovenia or PROPER). The named party's natural response is to dispute it. Where the amount or the "staff behaviour" claim is contestable, expect counter-claims, posts in residents' WhatsApp groups, and possible defamation complaints.
- **The audience is committee members of other client societies,** who often know each other through local RWA federations. A listing that looks unfair to a neighbouring committee may lower *their* trust in FirsThing, not only the named society's.
- **The "staff behaviour" criterion is subjective and one-sided.** It is the riskiest to publish, because it rests on FirsThing's own executives' reports.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found on churn, retaliation or word of mouth after a supplier publicly named its own B2B customers.
- Indian defamation and consumer-protection exposure from publishing a customer as a "defaulter" was not researched here. The Indian Penal Code successor (BNS) defamation provisions and contract confidentiality clauses need legal review.

## 5. Collective entities: shaming an organisation vs an individual (rotating volunteer committees)

### Takeaway
Public ratings of organisations can work — PROPER, Indonesia; Green Rating Project, India — but the response is concentrated among the worst performers and among organisations exposed to reputational pressure from their own stakeholders. No evidence was found on volunteer-run collectives with rotating leadership.

### Cited Findings
- **[NE/Obs] PROPER, Indonesia.** Five colour ratings, gold to black — [García, Sterner & Afsah, RFF/EfD](https://www.rff.org/publications/working-papers/public-disclosure-of-industrial-pollutionthe-proper-approach-for-indonesia/); [World Bank WP](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/770561468038722316/pdf/514630WP0IND0P10Box342028B01PUBLIC1.pdf).
  - Plants responded positively, especially those with poor compliance records.
  - BOD/COD fell by an estimated 32%, and the response was immediate.
  - Pilot compliance rose from 35% to 51% between 1995 and 1997.
  - Attribution is debated because of confounding factors.
- **[Obs] Green Rating Project, India (Powers, Blackman, Lyon & Narain, ERE 2011).** The Centre for Science and Environment rated India's largest pulp and paper mills — [RFF](https://www.rff.org/publications/working-papers/does-disclosure-reduce-pollution-evidence-from-indias-greenrating-project/); [EfD](https://www.efdinitiative.org/publications/does-disclosure-reduce-pollution-evidence-indias-green-rating-project-0).
  - Pollution fell significantly among the dirty plants and not among the cleaner ones.
  - Plants in wealthier communities, and single-plant firms, responded more.
  - The rating was run by an NGO, not a regulator, and still produced effects. This is the closest Indian precedent for a non-state rater.
- **[NE] Slovenia.** The threat of naming moved corporations more (8.5%) than individuals (5%), most in reputation-sensitive industries — [CEPR DP13194](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP13194.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Who feels the reputational cost:**
  - A housing society's reputational cost falls on the committee, which changes yearly and may blame its predecessors. Members did not personally incur the default.
  - Residents (the "stakeholders" analogous to the wealthier communities in GRP) are the plausible pressure channel. But this portal is shown to *other* societies' committees, not to the named society's own residents. The audience that would actually pressure the committee is not the one seeing the list.
- **The committee that defaulted may already have handed over by publication time,** so naming may hit people who did not cause the delay. This argues for naming only defaults that persist under the current committee, after a private warning to it.

### Gaps
- No study was found on naming RWAs, housing co-operatives, condominium associations or other volunteer-run bodies.
- No evidence was found on how leadership rotation moderates reputational sanctions.

## 6. Private warning before public naming (threat of disclosure as the main lever)

### Takeaway
The best natural experiment (Slovenia) shows the announced threat produced most of the compliance gain. Publication added a small, fast-fading effect. Regulators commonly put a private warning step first (India IT's show-cause notice). A staged warning → named design is consistent with the evidence. It is also the design that yields most of the benefit at least reputational cost.

### Cited Findings
- **[NE] Slovenia.** The threat during the four-month pre-announcement cut debt by about 8.5% (corporations) and 5% (individuals). The actual list's added effect was "marginal … and tapers off quickly" — [VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/shaming-and-tax-compliance); [Hohenheim DP 2018-21](https://wiso.uni-hohenheim.de/fileadmin/einrichtungen/wiso/Forschungsdekan/Papers_BESS/dp_2018-21_online.pdf).
- **[RCT] US letters.** These warned of visibility on a delinquent list (a threat delivered privately) and moved small debtors — [NBER w21264](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21264).
- **[Weak] India Income Tax.** A show-cause notice asking why the name should not be published came before newspaper publication — [Moneylife](https://www.moneylife.in/article/it-department-publishes-names-of-big-tax-defaulters/41076.html).
- **[Obs] UK HMRC.** A threat nobody knows about does nothing; only 28% were aware of the scheme — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/evaluating-the-effectiveness-of-publishing-details-of-deliberate-defaulters).
- **[RCT meta] Deterrence content.** Messages stating consequences outperform morale messages by about 3.2 vs 1.4 percentage points over reminders — [ZEW](https://www.zew.de/PU86278).

### Inferences
Design choices the evidence favours, for the report writer:
1. **Make the rule known in advance and in writing** (agreement, invoice footer, reminder), so that the threat exists for every society.
2. **Send a private, dated warning to the current committee** before any listing, with the amount, the due date and the listing date. Give a chance to dispute.
3. **Name only objective, uncontested facts.** Use overdue invoices beyond a threshold after the warning; avoid subjective "staff behaviour" ratings.
4. **Set a short display window and remove the name promptly once paid.** (HMRC uses a maximum of 12 months.)
5. **Recognise against a standard, not by rank,** and show that most societies pay on time.
6. **Do not show peer rankings or a visible bottom.**

### Gaps
- No experiment was found that compares "threat only, never publish" with "threat then publish" on long-run compliance and relationship outcomes.
- No evidence was found on how long a private warning window should be.
